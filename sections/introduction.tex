\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Lithium-ion batteries have become a key energy-storage technology for electric
vehicles and other high-power applications because of their high energy density
and long cycle life~\cite{ref1}. Shortening charging time is important for
improving vehicle availability and user convenience, but aggressive charging
can increase heat generation, accelerate parasitic reactions, and aggravate
long-term degradation~\cite{ref2}. Fast-charging protocol design is therefore
not a single-objective problem: charging speed must be balanced against thermal
stress and battery aging while operational constraints are respected.

Optimization-based charging design provides a systematic way to explore these
trade-offs. When each protocol evaluation requires a physical cycling test or a
high-fidelity electrochemical simulation, however, the number of affordable
evaluations is limited. Bayesian optimization (BO) is well suited to this
regime because it constructs a probabilistic surrogate from previously evaluated
protocols and uses an acquisition function to select informative new
queries~\cite{ref3}. Its sample efficiency has motivated several studies on
battery fast charging. Attia \textit{et al.} demonstrated closed-loop optimization
of fast-charging protocols with machine learning~\cite{ref11}; Jiang
\textit{et al.} formulated minimum-time charging as a constrained BO
problem~\cite{ref12}; and Wang and Jiang extended this direction to
multi-objective fast-charging design using Chebyshev decomposition and
constrained BO~\cite{ref13}. More recently, multi-objective BO has also been used
to optimize battery degradation characteristics with early lifetime
prediction~\cite{ref14}. These studies establish BO as a promising tool for
sample-efficient charging design under expensive evaluations.

For multiple conflicting objectives, a common strategy is to decompose the
vector-valued problem into a sequence of scalar subproblems. ParEGO is a
representative approach that combines scalarization with a probabilistic
surrogate and a single-objective acquisition rule~\cite{ref4}. Such methods
retain the uncertainty-aware exploration--exploitation mechanism of BO while
progressively covering different Pareto trade-off directions. Nevertheless,
their search decisions are still driven primarily by the observations acquired
within the current optimization run and by fixed algorithmic priors. This is a
significant limitation in the low-data regime encountered at the beginning of
battery optimization, where domain knowledge about current-profile shape,
state-of-charge-dependent charging stress, and safety margins may be useful but
is not directly represented in the surrogate data.

Large language models (LLMs) offer a possible mechanism for introducing such
contextual prior knowledge. Recent work on LLM-assisted BO has shown that an LLM
can improve zero-shot warm starting and candidate generation when only a small
number of observations is available~\cite{ref10}. However, directly delegating
the next charging experiment to an LLM is undesirable in a constrained battery
optimization problem: an LLM does not provide the calibrated probabilistic
posterior used by BO, and its generated protocols may violate numerical or
physical constraints. The relevant question is therefore not whether an LLM
should replace BO, but whether its domain knowledge can be incorporated into BO
without surrendering the surrogate-based uncertainty model, acquisition logic,
and explicit constraint handling.

To address this question, this paper proposes LLMBO-MO, an LLM-augmented
multi-objective Bayesian optimization framework for lithium-ion battery
fast-charging protocol design. The framework introduces LLM knowledge at two
controlled touchpoints while retaining the Gaussian-process (GP) surrogate and
acquisition function as the numerical decision core. During initialization, the
LLM overgenerates candidate charging protocols, after which deterministic
validation, repair, deduplication, and diversity-aware portfolio selection are
applied before simulator evaluation. During BO iterations, the LLM receives the
current scalarization preference and optimization state and returns a preferred
point or region. Valid guidance is translated into a confidence-scaled,
posterior-covariance-based local adjustment of the GP mean used for acquisition;
invalid or unusable guidance is discarded and the optimizer falls back to the
unmodified acquisition rule. Multi-objective trade-offs are handled through
augmented Tchebycheff scalarization with a dispersed set of Riesz-energy weight
vectors.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item An LLM-augmented multi-objective BO architecture is developed in which
LLM knowledge enters only through initialization and local preference guidance,
while the GP surrogate, acquisition function, explicit feasibility checks, and
simulator feedback retain control of the final evaluation decision.

\item A constrained warm-start portfolio mechanism is introduced to convert an
overgenerated LLM candidate pool into a small initial design through boundary
validation, repair, duplicate removal, soft-risk penalties, and diversity-aware
selection. This mechanism uses LLM knowledge without treating unevaluated LLM
suggestions as objective observations.

\item A guarded point/region coupling mechanism is developed to map LLM
preferences into a confidence-scaled local GP-mean adjustment based on posterior
covariance. Structural validity and feasibility checks provide a fail-safe path
to the original acquisition rule when the guidance cannot be used reliably.

\item The framework is evaluated with SPMe-based electrochemical--thermal--aging
simulators using the Chen2020 and Ecker2015 parameterizations. Under matched
evaluation budgets, LLMBO-MO improves final hypervolume over the ParEGO
reference in the representative Chen2020 case and increases the five-seed mean
hypervolume on Ecker2015; ablation experiments further quantify the separate and
combined effects of warm starting and iteration-level LLM guidance.
\end{enumerate}
