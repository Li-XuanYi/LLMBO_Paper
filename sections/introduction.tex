\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Fast and reliable charging is a key requirement for the large-scale deployment
of lithium-ion batteries in electric vehicles and energy-storage systems.
Increasing charging current can shorten charging time, but it also intensifies
heat generation and degradation mechanisms, creating unavoidable trade-offs
among charging speed, thermal safety, and battery durability
~\cite{ref1,ref2,refYang}.
Because user priorities vary across operating scenarios, charging-process design
is naturally a constrained multiobjective optimization problem whose desired
output is a set of Pareto-nondominated protocols rather than a single schedule.

Accurate evaluation of candidate charging protocols is expensive.
Physical cycling experiments require substantial time and laboratory resources,
particularly when degradation is considered.
Electrochemical-model-based design reduces the need for repeated physical tests,
but high-fidelity electrochemical--thermal models still require numerical
solution of coupled dynamic equations for every candidate protocol.
Consequently, the number of model evaluations becomes a principal bottleneck in
optimization-based charging design.
Bayesian optimization (BO) has therefore been widely used for battery charging
because its probabilistic surrogate and acquisition function can locate
promising solutions with substantially fewer expensive evaluations than direct
search~\cite{ref3,ref11,ref12,ref14}.
For multiple charging objectives, decomposition-based multiobjective BO further
provides a practical way to approximate a Pareto set under a limited simulation
budget~\cite{ref13,refZhu}.
Gaussian-process-assisted evolutionary optimization has also demonstrated the
value of surrogate-assisted search for efficiently obtaining diverse charging
protocols~\cite{refGPA}.

Despite this sample efficiency, BO is data driven and is least informative at
the beginning of an optimization campaign.
When only a few evaluated protocols are available, the GP surrogate and the
subsequent acquisition search are strongly affected by the initial sample
locations; uninformative initialization can therefore spend a non-negligible
fraction of a tight simulation budget exploring regions that are known to be
unattractive from charging-domain knowledge.
Existing battery-optimization studies improve search efficiency using
mesh-adaptive strategies, digital twins, parallel acquisition, aging-model
priors, electrochemical constraints, and historical numerical experience
~\cite{refDong,refDigitalTwin,refBLASTS,refPrBO,refYoon}.
These approaches show that prior information can accelerate optimization, but
most of the injected knowledge is numerical, model based, or derived from
previous evaluations.
Qualitative knowledge about multistage charging geometry, sensible current
profiles, SOC allocation, and thermal--degradation trade-offs is more difficult
to encode directly in a conventional BO pipeline.

Large language models (LLMs) provide a flexible interface for converting such
textual knowledge into optimization suggestions.
LLAMBO demonstrated LLM-assisted warm starting, candidate generation, and
surrogate-related reasoning for BO~\cite{ref10}; subsequent work has explored
LLM-generated point or region preferences and selective use of inexpensive LLM
predictions~\cite{ref16,refLABO}.
Battery-domain LLM-assisted BO has also been investigated for parameter
identification~\cite{refKuaiLLMBO}, while broader studies indicate that the
usefulness of LLM priors depends strongly on alignment with the target
scientific domain~\cite{refSoberLLMBO}.
However, directly using an LLM as an unconstrained numerical optimizer is
undesirable for battery fast-charging design: generated protocols may violate
the protocol domain, and model-generated objective estimates need not be
consistent with the electrochemical--thermal simulator.
A useful LLM prior should therefore guide the expensive search without replacing
the numerical multiobjective optimization process or the battery model used to
evaluate candidate protocols.

Motivated by this requirement, this paper proposes \emph{LLMBO-MO}, a
multiobjective Bayesian optimization method for battery fast-charging protocol
design.
The method retains a decomposition-based numerical MOBO backbone---trade-off
weight sampling, objective transformation and scalarization, GP fitting,
expected-improvement optimization, simulator evaluation, and Pareto update---and
introduces LLM knowledge only at two bounded search interfaces.
Before sequential optimization, a screened and diversity-aware LLM-informed
initialization constructs a domain-informed initial portfolio.
During an early guidance window, a weight-conditioned LLM region can provide
additional restarts to the numerical acquisition optimizer; invalid or absent
guidance immediately falls back to the original acquisition search.
In both mechanisms, a protocol enters the optimization database only after
battery-model evaluation.

The main contributions are summarized as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{LLM-informed initialization for multiobjective BO:}
    a protocol-domain-screened and diversity-aware initialization strategy is
    developed to convert battery-domain textual knowledge into an informative
    initial design for the low-data stage of multiobjective Bayesian
    optimization.

    \item \textbf{Bounded weight-conditioned regional guidance:}
    a second LLM mechanism uses the current multiobjective decomposition weight
    and optimization state to propose a bounded search region for early
    acquisition optimization.  The region is used only to enrich numerical EI
    restarts, supports explicit abstention and fallback, and does not create
    surrogate observations.
\end{enumerate}
Both mechanisms obey the same design constraint: the LLM influences search
locations, whereas objective values, feasibility decisions, GP targets, and
Pareto relations are determined only from simulator-evaluated protocols.
Numerical studies on Chen2020 and Ecker2015 compare LLMBO-MO with ParEGO and
representative expensive multiobjective optimizers, and mechanism studies assess
the two LLM guidance components separately.
