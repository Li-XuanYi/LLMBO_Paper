\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper addressed the challenge of integrating large-language-model (LLM)
knowledge into multiobjective Bayesian optimization for battery fast-charging
design, where evaluation budgets are tightly limited by the cost of
electrochemical--thermal simulation.
We proposed LLMBO-MO, a framework built on the insight that LLM-derived
preferences can guide \emph{where} the optimizer searches, provided that only
simulator-evaluated protocols determine \emph{what} enters the Pareto set.
This separation is enforced through an asymmetric two-touchpoint architecture:
a protocol-domain-screened warm-start that supplies diverse initial candidates
without per-cell calibration, and bounded regional guidance whose influence is
clipped to a fixed fraction of the posterior standard deviation and reduces
exactly to standard expected improvement when validation fails.

Under a matched budget of 56 simulator evaluations and five fixed seeds,
LLMBO-MO yielded consistent hypervolume improvement over ParEGO on two SPMe
parameterizations, with per-seed wins in three of five seeds on Chen2020 and
four of five on Ecker2015.
A component study further revealed that the screened LLM warm-start alone
accounts for the majority of the performance gain, while bounded regional
guidance provides a smaller but systematic complementary benefit.
The combined effect of the two touchpoints was approximately additive,
suggesting that they operate through largely independent mechanisms.

The practical message of this study is that language-model guidance can be
safely incorporated into data-scarce engineering optimization when the
interface enforces three constraints: the LLM never supplies training targets,
every advisory signal is bounded in magnitude, and any invalid advice triggers
an exact fallback to the unguided procedure.
This conservative design pattern is not specific to battery charging and may
apply to other domains where expensive simulators or physical experiments
define the evidence standard.

Several limitations define the scope of the present findings.
The evidence is simulation-based and uses a trajectory-averaged degradation
proxy rather than calibrated capacity fade measurements.
The comparison is centered on ParEGO; matched-budget evaluations against direct
hypervolume methods (qEHVI, qNEHVI) and surrogate-assisted evolutionary
algorithms remain to be conducted.
Two parameter sets with five seeds each provide initial evidence of
consistency, but broader validation across cell chemistries, aging states, and
LLM backends is needed to establish generality.
Physical-cell experiments are the natural next step for translating the
observed simulation-level gains into engineering practice.

Future work will pursue four directions: (i)~matched-budget comparisons with
direct hypervolume acquisition functions to position LLMBO-MO within the wider
multiobjective BO landscape; (ii)~controlled physical replay of selected Pareto
protocols on the laboratory platform to assess simulation-to-hardware
transferability; (iii)~systematic comparison of LLM backends under identical
optimization conditions to quantify how model capability affects guidance
quality; and (iv)~extension of the bounded-advisory design pattern to related
battery optimization tasks, including formation protocol design and
state-of-health-adaptive charging.
